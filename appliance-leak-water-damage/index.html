Appliance Leak Water Damage in Union City: What to Know

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. Washer supply hoses can burst and keep spraying at full pressure until the valve is closed. Dishwashers usually leak a little at a time, at the drain hose or door seal, hidden beneath the cabinets. The ice maker line on a refrigerator is a thin tube, and it can drip for months behind the unit. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

Our Union City crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Right Now

What to Do Right Now

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineFor a washing machine, close both the cold and hot valves on the wall at its back. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. The small valve for an ice maker line sits behind the refrigerator or underneath.
  3. Mop Up What You CanMop or towel up what you can reach so it stops spreading under cabinets and flooring. Vacuums are safe for this only when built for wet and dry pickup.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.

Insurance

Does Insurance Cover It?

A hose that bursts or a machine that overflows without warning is the kind of sudden and accidental event homeowners policies often cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

Common Causes

Why This Happens

  • Water under a refrigerator from a blocked defrost drain or a drain pan with a crack in it.
  • Ice maker lines, copper or plastic, that crack, kink or come loose where they connect.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
  • The water inlet valve on a washer or dishwasher failing to close, which lets the machine overfill.
  • Washer supply hoses made of rubber that get weak over the years and burst from the pressure.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

The ceiling under an upper floor laundry catches whatever water makes it past the floor above. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.

Kitchen Cabinets

Toe kicks and cabinet sides take on water from underneath when a dishwasher leaks. Panels swell and come apart, and a wet cabinet box can keep the wall behind it damp for weeks.

Rooms Next Door

A washer hose can release water for hours. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Hidden Mold at the Back

The warm, dark space behind a refrigerator or dishwasher rarely gets checked. Long before it is discovered, a slow drip back there can be growing mold across the floor and wall.
